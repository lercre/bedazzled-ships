Concise Fun Summary (Script-Ready)

  • The Problem: German U-boats were sinking ships at a terrifying rate. You can’t hide a ship at sea (smoke, wake, size).
  • The Solution (1917): Artist + Royal Naval Volunteer Reserve lieutenant Norman Wilkinson said: Don’t hide them — confuse the enemy. Paint wild geometric patterns so U-boat captains misjudge course, speed, and range through their periscopes.
  • Scale: 4,000+ British merchant ships + ~400 naval vessels. US Navy painted 1,200+ (“razzle dazzle”). Every ship unique.
  • How it looked: Bold stripes, zigzags, diamonds, false bow waves in black/white, blues, greens. Counterintuitive and highly visible.
  • Famous ships: RMS Mauretania (diamond checkers), RMS Olympic (Titanic’s sister), USS Leviathan, Kil-class gunboats like HMS Kildangan.
  • Even the King was fooled: King George V looked at a model through a periscope and got the direction completely wrong.
  • Picasso: Claimed Cubism invented it when he saw a camouflaged cannon (“C’est nous qui avons fait ça!”).
  • Did it work? Debated. Modest perceptual effect + morale boost. Largely obsolete once radar arrived. British analysis inconclusive; US numbers looked better.
  • Mandela Effect angle: Many people have zero memory of these wildly painted ships. “They were always plain gray!” Photos and records prove otherwise. Perfect “why doesn’t anyone remember this?” hook.

Full Report

History & Invention

By early 1917 unrestricted submarine warfare was crippling Allied shipping. Traditional low-visibility camouflage failed. Wilkinson proposed the opposite: high-visibility patterns designed to disrupt the visual form of the ship and make accurate torpedo aiming harder. The first trial ship was HMS Industry. A dedicated Dazzle Section was established at the Royal Academy of Arts. Designs were tested on scale models viewed through periscopes. Wilkinson was later officially recognized as the inventor.

Purpose & Design

Dazzle was never meant to make ships invisible. It was meant to make them hard to “read.” U-boat commanders had limited time and imperfect optics. A few degrees of error in estimated course or speed could cause a torpedo to miss. Patterns broke up the hull outline, created false bow waves, and made it difficult to tell which end was the bow. Each ship received a unique scheme.

Scale & Notable Ships

British totals exceeded 4,000 merchant ships and roughly 400 warships. The United States painted more than 1,200 vessels in 1918. Iconic examples include the RMS Mauretania, the RMS Olympic, the USS Leviathan, and Kil-class patrol boats such as HMS Kildangan.

Effectiveness

Contemporary analysis found no clear major combat advantage, though dazzle ships that were hit were somewhat less likely to sink and the paint jobs improved crew morale. Modern studies confirm modest perceptual distortions but the wartime benefit was limited. Radar later made pure visual dazzle largely obsolete.

The Mandela Effect Connection

Many people have no recollection of these ships despite extensive photographic evidence. The common reaction is “I never learned this” or “warships were always plain gray.” It is a documented historical practice that simply didn’t stick in popular memory.
